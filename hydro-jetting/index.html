Snaking Keeps Failing To Fix It?

A drain that clogs again within weeks of being snaked, especially a kitchen line or a main sewer line, usually has buildup or root intrusion coating the entire interior of the pipe, not just a single blockage point, and hydro jetting is the fix that actually removes it.

It's a more thorough approach than most homeowners realize exists, which is why a technician often recommends it only after a second or third snaking visit reveals the clog keeps returning.

Why Snaking Alone Doesn't Solve This

A mechanical snake pushes through or breaks up whatever's blocking the pipe at one specific point, which restores flow immediately but leaves the grease coating or root growth along the rest of the pipe wall untouched. That residue becomes the seed for the next clog, often within weeks.

How Hydro Jetting Works

A specialized nozzle sends water at 4,000 PSI or more through the full length of the pipe, scouring grease, scale, and root intrusion off the interior wall rather than just punching a path through the middle. The result is a pipe that's actually clean, not just temporarily clear.

What A Jetting Visit Looks Like

A technician first runs a camera to confirm the pipe's condition and identify blockage locations, then feeds the jetting hose to the far end of the line and works backward, flushing debris toward the access point rather than deeper into the system. A follow-up camera pass confirms the line is fully clear before the visit ends.

Why Jetting Comes Up More Often In Kennesaw's Older Lines

Clay and cast iron sewer lines common in Kennesaw's older neighborhoods have more interior joints than modern PVC, and each joint is a spot where roots and grease buildup tend to catch and accumulate. That's part of why a jetting service on an older line often reveals more buildup than the same service on a newer PVC line of similar age.

A standard jetting visit, including the before-and-after camera passes, typically takes one to two hours depending on the line's length and how much buildup has accumulated.

Signs You Need Jetting, Not Just Snaking

  • A drain clogs again within just a few weeks of being professionally snaked, suggesting the underlying buildup was never actually removed.
  • A kitchen line has a documented history of grease-related slow draining that keeps recurring despite regular use of the disposal.
  • A sewer line camera inspection shows visible root intrusion or heavy interior scale buildup along the pipe wall.

A drain clogging for the first time, without a history of recurring blockages, is more often a simple drain cleaning call. Root intrusion severe enough to have cracked the pipe itself needs the structural fix covered on the sewer line repair page. Frequently Asked Questions

Is hydro jetting safe for older pipes?
It's safe for most pipe materials in good structural condition, but a camera inspection beforehand is recommended on older lines to confirm there's no existing damage the pressure could worsen.
How often does a line need hydro jetting?
A kitchen line with heavy grease use might benefit from jetting every 1 to 2 years as preventive maintenance. Most residential lines don't need it unless there's a recurring clog problem.
Is hydro jetting more expensive than snaking?
Yes, but it addresses the underlying buildup rather than just clearing the immediate blockage, which often makes it the better value when clogs keep recurring.
Does hydro jetting damage pipes?
Properly calibrated pressure for the pipe's material and condition does not damage a structurally sound line; this is exactly why a camera inspection beforehand matters on older or already-compromised pipe.
Can hydro jetting be used on a kitchen sink line, not just the main sewer?
Yes, jetting works on branch lines as well as the main line, and is often the most effective fix for a kitchen line with chronic grease buildup.
Is hydro jetting a one-time fix or does it need repeating?
For a line with structural root intrusion, jetting clears it but the underlying crack remains, so periodic maintenance jetting or an eventual structural repair is still the long-term answer.
