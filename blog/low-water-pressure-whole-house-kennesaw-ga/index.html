Low Water Pressure Throughout Your Kennesaw, GA Home

By the Kennesaw Apex team

Low water pressure at a single fixture usually has a simple, localized cause. Low pressure throughout the entire house is a different problem, and it's worth understanding the distinction before assuming either one.

A Pressure Test Puts a Number On the Problem

Rather than relying on a subjective sense that pressure "feels low," a pressure gauge attached to an outdoor spigot gives an objective reading in PSI, which can be compared against the municipal supply's typical delivered pressure. Most residential systems run somewhere between 40 and 80 PSI. A reading well below that range confirms a real restriction rather than a perception issue, and gives a concrete baseline to compare against after any repair.

Ruling Out The Simple Explanations First

Before assuming a bigger problem exists, check whether the main shutoff valve and the water meter's own shutoff valve are both fully open, since a partially closed valve restricts flow noticeably to the entire house and is sometimes accidentally left partially closed after previous plumbing work. Also check with neighbors or the water utility for any known municipal supply issue or scheduled maintenance work, since a temporary utility-side problem produces the exact same symptom as something inside the home itself.

How Quickly Pressure Loss Actually Develops

Mineral and corrosion buildup inside aging pipe doesn't happen on a predictable timeline, but it's typically slow enough that a homeowner living in the same house for years won't notice week to week. It usually takes a specific event, a new low-flow showerhead making the difference suddenly obvious, or a visiting family member commenting on it, for someone to realize how much pressure has actually declined compared to what they'd simply gotten used to.

Sitewide Mineral Buildup In Older Pipes

In a home with original galvanized pipe, decades of internal corrosion gradually narrows the interior diameter of every supply line in the house, restricting flow evenly across every fixture. This is a gradual process, so pressure often drops slowly enough that homeowners don't notice until it's dropped substantially, sometimes over several years, which is exactly why a pressure gauge reading compared against a known baseline is more reliable than personal recollection of how strong the water used to feel.

A Failing Pressure Regulator

Many homes have a pressure-reducing valve where the main line enters the house, which regulates incoming municipal pressure down to a safer level for household plumbing. When this valve fails, it can restrict flow more than intended, producing sitewide low pressure even though the pipes themselves are in good condition. This is a relatively inexpensive, straightforward fix once identified.

A pressure regulator typically lasts 10 to 15 years before needing replacement, and unlike pipe corrosion, a failed regulator often produces a more sudden pressure drop rather than a gradual decline over years, which is a useful clue when trying to distinguish the two possible causes.

A Partially Closed Or Failing Main Valve

The main shutoff valve itself can develop mineral buildup or mechanical wear over years, particularly in an older home, preventing it from opening fully even when it appears to be in the open position. This produces the same sitewide restriction as a failing pressure regulator, and the two are sometimes confused until a technician tests each separately.

Comparing Sitewide vs. Single-Fixture Low Pressure

Sitewide vs. single-fixture pressure loss
PatternLikely Cause
Every fixture affected equallyPipe corrosion, valve issue, or pressure regulator
One fixture onlyClogged aerator, failing cartridge, or localized supply line issue
Pressure drops when another fixture runsUndersized supply line or partially closed valve somewhere in the system

A Water Softener Can Also Be The Culprit

A whole-home water softener sits inline with the main supply, and a softener with a clogged resin bed, a stuck valve, or one that's simply undersized for the household's peak demand can restrict flow enough to produce noticeable sitewide pressure loss. This is worth checking separately from pipe condition, since bypassing the softener temporarily (most units have a bypass valve) and checking whether pressure improves is a quick way to rule this cause in or out.

Why This Matters More In Older Kennesaw Homes

Homes with original 1980s-1990s copper or galvanized supply lines are more likely to see the gradual, corrosion-driven version of this problem, since decades of mineral buildup and, for galvanized pipe specifically, internal rust accumulation both restrict flow the same way. A home that's already been repiped with PEX rarely develops this issue from pipe condition alone, which is a useful diagnostic clue on its own.

How A Technician Actually Diagnoses This

A pressure gauge test at an outdoor spigot or the main line gives an objective reading compared to the expected municipal supply pressure, which quickly narrows down whether the problem is upstream, at the municipal supply or the main valve, or distributed throughout the home's own internal pipe network. From there, checking the pressure regulator's function and, if pipe corrosion is suspected, assessing overall pipe condition determines the real fix.

Kennesaw Note: Check Who Else Is Affected Before You Dig In

Cobb County Water System's customer guide puts the meter at the property edge and makes the owner responsible for piping and fixtures after it. If neighbors report the same drop, the cause may be on the utility side, and Cobb Water is the place to ask.

If only your house is affected, the problem sits on your side of the meter, and the usual suspects are a partly closed valve, a pressure regulator or corroded supply lines. During a hard freeze, Cobb Water has said that having no water at all is likely due to frozen pipes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can weather affect water pressure?
Extreme heat can occasionally affect municipal supply pressure during peak demand periods, but this is usually temporary and affects a whole neighborhood rather than a single home.
Is a pressure regulator required by code?
Many jurisdictions require one when incoming municipal pressure exceeds a certain threshold, both to protect household plumbing and appliances from excessive pressure.
Will replacing one corroded pipe section fix sitewide low pressure?
If the corrosion is widespread throughout the home's original pipe, a single section replacement won't resolve pressure at every other fixture. A full repipe addresses the sitewide pattern.
Who do I call when pressure drops in Kennesaw, Cobb Water or a plumber?
If neighbors have the same problem, start with Cobb Water. Its customer guide makes piping on the home side of the meter the owner's responsibility, so a drop that only affects your house is a plumbing question.
