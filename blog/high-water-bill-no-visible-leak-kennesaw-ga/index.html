High Water Bill With No Visible Leak? A Kennesaw, GA Guide

By the Kennesaw Apex team

A water bill that jumps thirty or fifty dollars with no visible puddle, no dripping faucet, and no obvious change in how the household uses water is one of the most common reasons Kennesaw homeowners call a plumber. The good news is that almost every case traces back to one of a handful of specific, checkable causes, not a mystery.

Start With The Meter Test

Before checking anything else, turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water in the house, including the ice maker line and any irrigation system, then go look at the water meter. If the meter's leak indicator (usually a small triangular or star-shaped dial) is spinning, or if the meter reading changes over a 15 to 20 minute period with everything off, there's an active leak somewhere on the home's side of the line.

This one test rules an entire category of causes in or out before you spend time checking anything else, and it takes less than half an hour.

Why This Deserves Real Attention, Not Just a Shrug

A high water bill is often treated as an annoyance to tolerate rather than a symptom worth investigating, but water waste of this scale, hundreds of gallons a day in a bad case, has real costs beyond the bill itself.

A leak significant enough to affect a water bill meaningfully is also large enough that, left long enough, it can contribute to foundation soil erosion, mold growth in a wall cavity, or damage to flooring, none of which show up as quickly as the bill does.

The Most Common Cause: A Running Toilet

A worn flapper that isn't sealing completely can leak 200 gallons or more per day without making any noticeable sound, which is exactly why it's the single most common cause of an unexplained high bill. Add a few drops of food coloring to each toilet tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and check whether color has appeared in the bowl. If it has, that toilet has a leak, and it's an inexpensive fix.

Irrigation Systems Are a Close Second

A cracked underground irrigation line or a valve that isn't closing fully can waste 500 gallons or more in a single watering cycle, and because the line is buried, there's often no visible sign at the surface at all. If the home has a sprinkler system, running it manually and watching each zone for unusually wet spots, weak pressure, or a zone that keeps running past its programmed time is worth checking before assuming the leak is inside the house.

Water Heater Leaks and Silent Drips

A water heater with a slow leak, whether from the tank itself or a loose fitting, can run for weeks before it's noticed, especially if the unit sits in a garage where a small amount of water evaporates or drains away without pooling visibly. Checking the floor around the base of the tank for staining or mineral deposits, even without standing water, catches this early.

What a Faulty Meter Actually Looks Like

A malfunctioning water meter is a real but less common cause, and it's worth ruling out last, after the more likely causes above. If the meter test in the first section showed no movement with everything off, but the bill still seems too high for actual usage, requesting a meter accuracy check from the water utility is the next step, since a plumber can't diagnose a faulty meter directly.

A Note On Seasonal Usage Changes

Before assuming a mechanical cause, it's worth ruling out a genuine seasonal shift in usage. Filling a pool, running an irrigation system more often during a hot, dry stretch, or hosting extended family for a few weeks can all produce a real, temporary bill increase that has nothing to do with a leak. Comparing the current bill to the same month a year earlier, rather than just the previous month, helps separate a seasonal pattern from a genuine new problem.

When It's Time To Call a Plumber

If the meter test shows continued movement with every fixture off, the leak is somewhere in the home's own plumbing, whether that's an underground line running to the house or a leak hidden inside a wall or under a slab. At that point, electronic leak detection equipment finds the exact location without guessing, which is the difference between a small, targeted repair and unnecessary demolition.

A leak that's been running long enough to meaningfully affect a water bill has usually been going for weeks already, so there's no real downside to getting it checked promptly once the simple checks above haven't found an obvious cause.

Kennesaw Note: Check Your Cobb Water Billing Dates First

In January 2023 Cobb Water said a problem with its meter-reading contractor delayed some bills and stretched the billing period, which the county said might mean a slightly higher bill than average. Before hunting for a leak, compare the dates on your statement with earlier ones.

If the period is normal and the usage still jumped, Cobb County Water System's customer guide says a sudden increase in water usage could indicate a leak. The same guide names faucets, toilets and irrigation systems as common leak locations, which makes them the first places to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a hidden leak usually run before someone notices the bill?
Often several weeks to a couple of months, since a slow leak inside a wall or under a slab produces no obvious daily sign until the bill arrives.
Can a bad water softener cause a high water bill?
Yes, a softener stuck in a regeneration cycle or with a malfunctioning valve can waste a significant amount of water without any visible sign.
Will the water company tell me if my meter is faulty?
Most utilities will test a meter on request if usage looks inconsistent with a customer's actual habits, though a plumber's leak detection is the faster first step if the meter test at home showed active movement.
Could a longer billing period explain a higher Cobb Water bill?
It can. In January 2023 Cobb Water said a meter-reading contractor problem delayed some bills and lengthened billing periods. Check the dates on your statement, and if the period looks normal, test the meter with everything off.
