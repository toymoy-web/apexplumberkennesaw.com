How Often Should You Really Flush Your Water Heater?

By the Kennesaw Apex team

Flushing a water heater is one of those maintenance tasks that's easy to skip year after year since nothing seems obviously wrong, right up until sediment buildup causes a real, expensive problem.

What Sediment Actually Is

Municipal water carries trace minerals, and over time those minerals settle to the bottom of a tank water heater as sediment. Cobb County's moderately hard water accelerates this process compared to areas with softer water. That sediment layer insulates the bottom of the tank from the heating element or gas burner beneath it, forcing the unit to work harder and longer to heat the same amount of water.

Gas vs. Electric Flush Procedure Differences

The flush process is nearly identical for gas and electric units, with one key difference: a gas unit's pilot light needs to be set to the lowest setting or turned off during the flush to avoid running the burner with no water in the tank, while an electric unit simply needs power disconnected at the breaker for the same reason, running heating elements dry can damage them within minutes.

The Real Recommended Interval

Most manufacturers recommend flushing a tank water heater once a year, though homes with notably hard water or a tank that's never been flushed at all may benefit from doing it slightly more often in the first year to clear out accumulated buildup, then settling into an annual schedule after that.

A Real Comparison: Flushed vs. Never-Flushed Tanks

A tank water heater that's been flushed annually for its entire service life typically reaches or exceeds its full rated 10 to 12 year lifespan without major incident. A comparable unit that's never been flushed often shows its first real symptom, reduced capacity, unusual sounds, or a failed heating element working overtime against insulating sediment, several years earlier, and is statistically more likely to fail at the tank itself rather than at an easily repaired component.

What Happens If It's Skipped For Years

Sediment buildup left unaddressed does more than just reduce efficiency. It can cause a popping or rumbling sound during heating cycles, as water trapped under the sediment layer boils and bubbles up through it. Over enough years, thick sediment buildup can also insulate the tank bottom unevenly, creating hot spots that stress the tank material and shorten its overall lifespan, sometimes by several years compared to a regularly flushed unit.

Signs Your Water Heater Is Overdue

  • A popping, cracking, or rumbling sound during the heating cycle.
  • Noticeably longer time to produce hot water than when the unit was newer.
  • It's been more than 12 months since the last flush, or the flush history is unknown.
  • Reduced hot water capacity compared to the tank's rated size.

A Practical Reminder Worth Setting

Given how easy this maintenance task is to forget, setting a recurring annual reminder tied to a memorable date, a birthday, the start of a season, or a home's move-in anniversary, is a simple way to keep a water heater on schedule without relying on memory alone. Some homeowners simply schedule it alongside another annual home maintenance task, like changing HVAC filters seasonally, to make it part of an existing routine rather than a separate thing to remember.

Tankless Units Have a Different but Related Issue

A tankless water heater doesn't accumulate sediment in a tank, since there is no tank, but it does accumulate mineral scale on its internal heat exchanger through the same hard water exposure. That's addressed through a descale flush rather than a sediment flush, typically recommended every 12 to 18 months in the Kennesaw area, and it's a different service than tank flushing even though the underlying cause, hard water minerals, is the same.

What A Flush Actually Looks Like Step By Step

The process starts with turning off power to an electric unit or setting a gas unit to pilot mode, then connecting a garden hose to the drain valve near the bottom of the tank and running the other end to a floor drain or outside. With the cold water supply valve closed and a hot water fixture opened elsewhere in the house to relieve pressure, the drain valve is opened and the tank empties completely, carrying sediment out with it.

Once the tank is empty, briefly opening the cold water supply again flushes any remaining sediment before closing the drain valve, refilling the tank, and restoring power or returning the gas unit to its normal setting. The whole process typically takes 30 to 45 minutes from start to finish.

Can This Be Done as a DIY Task?

A basic flush, connecting a hose to the tank's drain valve and running water through until it runs clear, is within reach for a comfortable DIY homeowner. That said, a professional flush also includes checking the anode rod, testing the pressure relief valve, and inspecting for early signs of tank corrosion, none of which get caught by a simple drain-and-refill. For a tank that's never been professionally serviced, having a technician handle the first flush catches problems a DIY approach would miss entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I never flush my water heater at all?
Sediment accumulates continuously, gradually reducing efficiency and hot water capacity, and increasing the odds of a popping sound and, eventually, a shortened tank lifespan.
Does flushing a water heater use a lot of water?
No, the flush itself only uses the water already in the tank, drained out and refreshed; it doesn't add meaningfully to a water bill.
Can flushing an old, never-serviced tank cause it to start leaking?
In rare cases, disturbing years of built-up sediment in a very old tank can reveal or worsen existing corrosion; a technician can assess this risk before flushing a tank that's never been serviced.
